\begin{abstract}
Late-interaction retrieval over visual documents (ColPali-family models) stores
hundreds of patch vectors per page, making storage the dominant deployment
cost. We present \bitmax{}, a library of exact CUDA scoring kernels for
compressed multi-vector MaxSim, and a systematically measured frontier of five
compression tiers spanning $8\times$ to $96\times$ compression versus fp32
storage. On the full ViDoRe benchmark (10 datasets, complete test splits,
8{,}443 queries), a symmetric int4 format scored by a dp4a int8-query kernel
retains dense-fp16 quality within $-0.0018$ \ndcg{} (95\% CI
$[-0.0030,-0.0006]$) at $8\times$ compression, and 1-bit sign quantization
retains it within $-0.0052$ at $32\times$. Our central empirical finding is
that \emph{the tier ranking reorders with corpus scale}: per-token magnitude
side-information significantly improves quality on small corpora
($\le$\,500 documents) but significantly \emph{hurts} beyond
$10^4$ documents, while token-pooled binary representations strengthen with
scale --- at 10{,}171 and 25{,}000 documents, a $3\times$-pooled binary tier at
$95.9\times$ compression outperforms unpooled binary and matches a
PLAID-style baseline's recall@10 at $28\times$ less storage, answering 256
queries in 0.46\,s on one RTX~4090. We replicate the tier ordering on a second
embedding model, correct a $27\times$ latency inflation in a na\"ive dense
baseline before citing speedups, and report six measured negative results.
All kernels are parity-tested, and every number derives from committed JSON
artifacts with stored per-query metrics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Late-interaction retrieval~\citep{khattab2020colbert} scores a query against a
document as a sum of per-query-token maxima over document token vectors
(MaxSim). Applied to visual documents, ColPali-family
models~\citep{faysse2024colpali} embed each page as roughly a thousand
128-dimensional patch vectors, so a corpus of only $10^4$ pages already
occupies gigabytes at fp32 --- storage, not compute, dominates deployment.

Compression of multi-vector representations is usually studied one technique
at a time and on a single corpus size. Practitioners face a different
question: \emph{given a corpus, which compression tier should be the default,
and what exactly does each step of compression cost?} This paper answers that
question empirically for visual late-interaction retrieval, with three
contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A measured five-tier frontier} --- binary signs ($32\times$),
binary with fp16 or 4-bit-log per-token scales ($28.4\times$/$31\times$),
symmetric int4 ($8\times$), and token-pooled binary
($63.9\times$/$95.9\times$) --- evaluated jointly on quality, storage, and
wall-clock latency with exact, parity-tested CUDA kernels, including a dp4a
int8-query kernel that accelerates int4 scoring $4.2\times$ with bit-identical
ranking.
\item \textbf{A scale-dependence finding} (Figure~\ref{fig:scaleflip}):
per-token magnitude restoration is a \emph{small-corpus} optimization --- a
significant gain at $\le$\,500 documents that inverts into a significant loss
at $10^4$+ documents --- while token pooling improves relatively with scale,
to the point that a $95.9\times$-compressed pooled tier beats uncompressed-bit
binary at 10k and 25k documents. Tier defaults should be corpus-size-aware.
\item \textbf{Measurement hygiene results} that materially change headline
claims: a vectorized dense baseline (score-identical to the na\"ive
per-document loop) is $27\times$ faster, deflating ``speedup vs.\ dense''
claims accordingly; and six attractive-but-ineffective techniques are
documented with evidence, including two fully implemented CUDA kernels that
lost to simpler baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Late interaction and PLAID.} ColBERT~\citep{khattab2020colbert} and
ColBERTv2~\citep{santhanam2022colbertv2} introduced token-level late
interaction and residual compression; PLAID~\citep{santhanam2022plaid}
accelerates it with centroid interaction. ColPali~\citep{faysse2024colpali}
transfers the paradigm to document images. We compare against
\texttt{fast-plaid}, a modern PLAID-style implementation, and dense fp16
MaxSim as the quality ceiling.

\textbf{Vector compression.} Binary sign quantization with asymmetric scoring
(float query $\times$ binary document) is classical~\citep{jacob2018quant}, and
per-vector scale restoration relates to product-quantization residual ideas
\citep{jegou2011pq}. Learned rotations (ITQ~\citep{gong2013itq}) reduce
binarization error for symmetric Hamming retrieval; we find they do not
transfer robustly to asymmetric MaxSim (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).

\textbf{Token pruning and pooling.} Reducing the number of stored vectors per
document via clustering has been explored for ColBERT-style
models~\citep{clavie2024pooling}. We measure hierarchical mean-pooling at
factors 2--3 jointly with bit-level quantization and find the combination
--- not pooling alone --- defines the extreme end of the frontier.

\section{Compression Tiers and Kernels}

\subsection{Storage formats}
All tiers store per-document token matrices $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n_d \times 128}$ under MaxSim scoring $s(q,d)=\sum_i \max_j \langle q_i, x_j\rangle$.

\textbf{Binary (32$\times$).} $x_j \mapsto \mathrm{sign}(x_j)$ packed to 16
bytes per token; scoring is asymmetric (fp32 query against $\pm1$ documents).

\textbf{Per-token scales (28.4$\times$/31$\times$/30.1$\times$).} Binary signs
plus one magnitude $\sigma_j = \mathrm{mean}|x_j|$ per token, applied to the
dot product \emph{before} the per-query-token max. Scales are stored as fp16
(2 bytes), 16 log-spaced levels (4 bits, \texttt{u4}), or 256 log-spaced
levels (1 byte, \texttt{u8}). Sixteen levels are statistically
indistinguishable from fp16 (paired $\Delta = -0.00003$, $p{=}1.0$ over
8{,}443 queries), so \texttt{u4} strictly dominates \texttt{u8}.

\textbf{Symmetric int4 (8$\times$).} Two signed 4-bit values per byte with one
per-tensor scale. A per-token int4 scale variant was evaluated in exact-kernel
simulation and rejected ($-0.011$ \ndcg{}).

\textbf{Pooled binary (63.9$\times$/95.9$\times$).} Ward clustering of each
document's tokens on the unit sphere to $\lceil n_d/f \rceil$ clusters
($f\in\{2,3\}$), mean-pooled then sign-quantized. Pooling composes poorly with
per-token scales (Section~\ref{sec:negative}); the pooled tiers are pure
binary.

\subsection{CUDA kernels}
Documents are GPU-resident (packed bits, offsets, optional scale vectors).
Three scoring kernels are routed by corpus shape: a generic bit-unpack kernel;
an unrolled dim-128 kernel, which a tokens-per-document gate (threshold 256,
determined by a warm-up$+$median microbenchmark sweep) extends from its
original $\le$128-document restriction to all long-document corpora for a
$\sim$2$\times$ gain at every corpus size tested; and a dp4a kernel for int4
that quantizes queries to int8 on-device (per-token symmetric scales, applied
after the max --- rank-safe), permutes them to match the nibble interleave,
and accumulates with \texttt{\_\_dp4a}, yielding $4.2\times$ over fp32-query
int4 scoring with quality identical to four decimals. All kernels preserve
exact empty-document semantics and deterministic lower-index tie-breaking,
verified by parity tests against CPU references.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Data.} The ViDoRe benchmark: DocVQA, InfoVQA, ArxivQA, TabFQuAD,
TAT-DQA, and five SyntheticDocQA domains at full test splits (6{,}729 unique
pages, 8{,}443 queries); a 10{,}171-document unique mixed corpus assembled
from fourteen ViDoRe sources (256 held-out queries); and a 25{,}000-document
stress corpus. Documents and queries are embedded with
\texttt{vidore/\allowbreak colqwen2-v1.0-hf} (dim 128); a four-dataset replication
uses \texttt{vidore/\allowbreak colpali-v1.3-hf}. Embeddings are built at batch size~1: we
found the reference builder is \emph{not batch-faithful} (batch-4 embeddings
correlate with batch-1 at mean cosine $0.19$), so batch-1 is load-bearing;
throughput is recovered by running many single-page builder processes
concurrently on one large-memory GPU, which preserves fidelity exactly.

\textbf{Measurement.} All retrieval measurements use one RTX~4090. Quality
runs use repeat-3 batched scoring with per-query \ndcg{} vectors persisted for
paired analysis; kernel-level latency claims use warm-up$+$median
microbenchmarks. The dense fp16 reference is computed two ways: the na\"ive
per-document loop found in many harnesses, and a vectorized implementation
(one flat fp16 matmul per query plus a segment-max, validated score-identical).
The loop implementation overstates dense latency $27\times$ (166\,s vs.\
6.04\,s at 10k documents); \emph{all speedups below are relative to the
vectorized baseline}. Statistical claims use paired per-query deltas pooled
over the suite with $10^4$-sample bootstrap CIs and two-sided sign tests.

\subsection{The tier ranking reorders with corpus scale}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.8\linewidth]{figures/scale_flip.pdf}
\caption{Quality of each tier relative to plain binary across four corpus
scales. Token-scale tiers win only on the smallest corpus; pooled tiers
strengthen monotonically with scale. Each point compares methods on an
identical corpus; corpora differ between points.}
\label{fig:scaleflip}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:scaleflip} is the central result. On a 256-document corpus,
fp16 token scales recover 86\% of the binary-to-dense gap ($+0.0092$ over
binary). Pooled over the 0.5--1.7k-document suite, the same tier is already
\emph{significantly below} plain binary ($-0.0015$, $p{=}0.003$), and at
10{,}171 documents the deficit grows to $-0.0059$. Pooling shows the opposite
trend: pool3 costs $-0.0133$ versus binary at 256 documents but \emph{gains}
$+0.0112$ at 10k and $+0.0238$ at 25k. We attribute the token-scale reversal
to magnitude heterogeneity across a mixed corpus --- high-norm tokens from
foreign documents win per-query-token maxima that sign information alone
would not grant them --- and pooling's relative strength at scale to its
denoising of near-duplicate patches, which grow more confusable as the corpus
grows. Practical consequence: \textbf{compression defaults should be selected
by corpus size}, and small-slice benchmarks actively mislead tier selection.

\subsection{Generality across embedding models}
Replicating four datasets with ColPali-v1.3 preserves the tier ordering
(dense 0.8208 mean; int4$+$dp4a 0.8122; binary 0.8069; tiers within 0.015),
indicating the frontier is a property of the representation family rather
than one model.

\section{Negative Results}
\label{sec:negative}

Six techniques were implemented, measured, and rejected; we report them
because each looks attractive on paper. (i)~\emph{dp4a for the binary path}:
quality-clean but 5--17\% slower --- the unrolled fp32 kernel is
bandwidth-bound, so ALU savings cannot pay for the quantization pass.
(ii)~\emph{One-pass query-tiled scoring}: $\sim$9$\times$ slower from 12\,MB
to 300\,MB corpora; per-block document rows are already L1-resident across
query tokens, and tiling moves query operands from registers to shared
memory. (iii)~\emph{ITQ rotations}: pooled-tier deltas of
$+0.006/+0.003/-0.005/-0.007$ across four 256-query slices --- per-corpus sign
flips; large small-slice gains (up to $+0.047$) did not replicate.
(iv)~\emph{Scale refinements} (tempering $\sigma^\alpha$, quantile clipping,
centroid combinations, salience-aware and similarity-threshold pooling):
all neutral-to-worse; plain mean-absolute scales are a local optimum.
(v)~\emph{Cluster-mass-weighted pooled scales}: catastrophic (\ndcg{}
0.29--0.80) --- under MaxSim, large background clusters must not amplify their
match scores. (vi)~\emph{Per-token int4 scales}: $-0.011$ \ndcg{} in
exact-semantics simulation.

\section{Limitations}
All latency measurements use one GPU model (RTX 4090); kernel-level findings
(memory-boundedness of the binary path, dp4a gains) may shift on other
architectures. Per-query significance data covers the suite but not the 10k
comparison (256 queries there). The fast-plaid latency instability we observed
deserves its own investigation before any comparative latency claim. Our
corpora are visual documents; transfer to text-only ColBERT corpora is
unverified. The scale-dependence mechanism is supported but not proven by our
attribution analysis.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}
Every reported number derives from a JSON artifact committed under
\texttt{docs/\allowbreak benchmark\_results/\allowbreak raw/} in the
\bitmax{} repository, including per-query metric vectors for all suite rows.
Paper figures regenerate deterministically from those artifacts
(\texttt{paper/\allowbreak make\_figures.py}); the statistical analysis is
\texttt{benchmarks/\allowbreak significance.py} with a fixed seed. Embedding caches
rebuild via \texttt{benchmarks/\allowbreak reproduce.py} recipes at batch size~1; kernel
correctness is enforced by the \texttt{pytest -m cuda} parity suite. Total
compute for all experiments in this paper was approximately 30 RTX-4090-hours
and 1 H100-hour of rented capacity.

\section{Conclusion}
Compressed late-interaction retrieval is not a single trade-off but a family
of them, and the right choice moves with corpus size. A practitioner can hold
dense quality to within $0.002$ \ndcg{} at $8\times$ compression
(int4$+$dp4a), or accept $\sim$$0.01$ at $96\times$ (pool3) --- at 5--13$\times$
the speed of an honestly-implemented dense baseline on the same GPU. All
kernels, benchmarks, artifacts, and negative results are released with the
library.
